summary: "Why concrete the Romans poured into the sea two thousand years ago is still standing, and what modern builders are learning from it."

Take a walk along almost any modern coastline and you will see twentieth-century engineering slowly falling apart. Seawalls crumble, bridge supports flake away, and concrete piers rust from the inside out. Most of these structures need major, expensive repairs within fifty to eighty years of being built.

Two thousand years ago, Roman engineers poured their own concrete, known as opus caementicium, straight into the Mediterranean Sea. They built breakwaters, harbours, and vaults that have survived two millennia of crashing waves and seawater while actually growing stronger over time. The gap between ancient durability and modern fragility comes down to basic chemistry, kiln temperatures, and what our economies choose to reward. Modern concrete is built for speed and quick returns, while Roman concrete was designed to settle into a lasting chemical balance with its environment.

Almost everything we build today relies on Ordinary Portland Cement, first patented in 1824 by a Leeds bricklayer named Joseph Aspdin and refined into its modern high-temperature form by his son William a couple of decades later. To make it, factories heat limestone and clay in a massive rotating kiln to around 1,450°C. This creates hard lumps called clinker, which are ground into a fine grey powder alongside a small amount of gypsum. When mixed with water, the powder sets quickly into artificial stone.

On its own, concrete is brilliant at handling heavy weights pushing straight down on it, which engineers call compressive strength. However, it is terrible at handling stretching or bending forces, known as tensile strength. Try to bend a slab of plain concrete and it will snap cleanly in half. To stop modern buildings and bridges from snapping, builders embed a skeleton of steel bars inside the wet concrete.

That pairing of concrete and steel creates a built-in self-destruct mechanism. Fresh Portland cement is naturally very alkaline, with a pH between 12.5 and 13.5. That high alkalinity forms a thin, protective chemical film around the steel bars and stops them from rusting. Over a few decades, carbon dioxide from the air slowly seeps into the microscopic pores of the concrete, gradually lowering its pH and stripping away that shield. Near the coast or on roads treated with winter grit, salt ions speed up the damage by attacking the metal directly.

Once the steel starts to rust, it expands to between two and six times its original volume. All that swelling pushes outward with enormous force, cracking the surrounding concrete from the inside. Those cracks let in even more water and oxygen, accelerating the rust until chunks of concrete break off completely (Broomfield, 2007).

Roman marine concrete avoided this trap because it used a completely different chemical recipe and had no metal inside it. As the Roman architect Vitruvius recorded in his manual De Architectura around 30 to 15 BCE, Roman builders mixed lime with volcanic ash, specifically a variety called pozzolana from the area around Naples, alongside chunks of volcanic rock or broken brick. Instead of setting through a fast reaction with fresh water, Roman concrete cured through a slow chemical reaction between lime, seawater, and volcanic glass.

For years, scientists assumed the volcanic ash alone explained why Roman buildings lasted so long. Yet whenever modern researchers tried to recreate the recipe using standard slaked lime (lime pre-mixed with water into a paste), the concrete never developed the same self-healing abilities.

In 2023, a team led by Admir Masic at MIT uncovered the real mechanism: "hot mixing" (Seymour et al., 2023). The Romans mixed dry, reactive quicklime directly with the volcanic ash and water. That combination triggered a fierce chemical reaction that generated intense heat, leaving behind tiny, white, chalky specks throughout the concrete called lime clasts. For decades, archaeologists assumed those white specks were just signs of sloppy mixing. In reality, they work like tiny emergency repair kits. Whenever a hairline crack forms in the concrete, rainwater or seawater seeps in and dissolves the calcium-rich specks. That creates a mineral-rich liquid that either hardens into fresh calcium carbonate to plug the gap, or reacts with the surrounding volcanic ash to glue the crack shut.

When Roman concrete sits underwater, the chemistry gets even more impressive. Using high-powered X-rays to inspect core samples drilled from ancient Roman harbours, geologist Marie Jackson and her team found that seawater actually toughens the concrete as the centuries pass (Jackson et al., 2017).

As seawater slowly dissolves the glassy particles in the volcanic ash, it sparks the growth of rare, interlocking crystals, specifically aluminous tobermorite and phillipsite, inside the tiny pores of the concrete. These plate-like crystals weave together like microscopic fibres, stopping cracks from spreading and making the concrete much harder to fracture. In modern concrete, seawater washes away binding chemicals and corrodes the steel inside. In Roman concrete, seawater acts as the very ingredient that keeps the structure curing and healing.

If Roman concrete is so tough and repairs itself, why did we abandon it? The answer comes down to construction speed, economics, and a major chemical clash with modern steel.

First, Roman concrete takes weeks or even months to reach full strength, and its final compressive strength is lower than modern structural mixes. Modern construction runs on tight schedules where contractors want to pour concrete, strip away the wooden moulds within days, and move straight onto the next floor.

Second, there is the problem of tensile strength and steel reinforcement. Like all concrete, the Roman mix struggles with stretching and bending forces. Roman engineers worked around this through geometry, building thick, heavy structures like arches and vaults that kept all the weight pushing straight down. The unreinforced concrete dome of the Pantheon in Rome still stands after nearly two thousand years because its walls are several metres thick at the base, tapering towards the top using lightweight volcanic pumice to keep the weight down.

Today, we want slim skyscrapers and long, sweeping bridges, which require internal reinforcement. You might think we could simply pour Roman concrete around modern steel bars, but the chemistry backfires. The pozzolanic reaction uses up the excess calcium hydroxide that keeps modern concrete highly alkaline, leaving Roman concrete with a much lower pH (closer to 10 or 11). If you embed standard carbon steel bars inside it, that lower alkalinity fails to maintain a protective film over the metal, causing the steel to rust even faster than usual.

Finally, there is simple geography. Good volcanic ash is only found in specific volcanic regions, while limestone and clay can be dug up almost anywhere on Earth.

Even so, turning two-thousand-year-old chemistry into something a modern building contractor can actually buy is no longer just a laboratory curiosity, and several research teams and startups are already doing the groundwork. Following his 2023 MIT study on hot mixing, materials scientist Admir Masic co-founded a company called DMAT alongside engineer Paolo Sabatini to bring self-healing Roman concrete back onto commercial building sites. Their goal is to adapt the quicklime-clast recipe so it sets fast enough for modern construction schedules while keeping those tiny calcium repair pockets intact. By tweaking the mix for precast blocks, seawalls, and bridge supports, they are trying to give modern builders concrete that seals its own hairline cracks before water can do lasting damage.

Other companies are solving the volcanic ash problem without needing a volcano nearby. Because good pozzolana is hard to source globally, firms like Urban Mining Industries in the United States have turned to an everyday waste product: crushed bottle glass. Post-consumer glass has almost the exact same glassy silica and alumina makeup as ancient volcanic ash. When ground into a fine powder and blended into concrete, it triggers the same slow pozzolanic reaction, soaking up excess calcium hydroxide and sealing off microscopic pores so seawater struggles to get inside.

Even the best Roman-inspired concrete still needs internal reinforcement to carry heavy loads across long spans, which is where volcanic rock makes a second appearance. Over the past decade, structural engineers and manufacturers such as ReforceTech in Norway and Technobasalt have been scaling up basalt fibre reinforced polymer (BFRP) rebar. To make it, factories melt crushed volcanic basalt rock at around 1,400°C, draw the molten stone into continuous, hair-thin fibres, and bind them together with resin to form ribbed bars that look a lot like standard rebar.

Basalt rebar weighs roughly a quarter as much as steel, handles higher pulling forces before snapping, and completely ignores salt and moisture. Because basalt bars never rust, they do not need the highly alkaline environment that Portland cement provides to protect steel. That makes basalt reinforcement a natural match for lower-pH, Roman-style pozzolanic concrete. Civil engineers in coastal regions, including transport departments in Florida and marine infrastructure researchers at universities like Queen's University Belfast, have already tested and installed basalt-reinforced concrete in seawalls, bridge decks, and coastal piers where salt spray normally ruins steel bars within a single generation.

Alongside Roman chemical recipes, researchers are also borrowing tricks from biology to heal cracks. At Delft University of Technology in the Netherlands, microbiologist Henk Jonkers developed a self-healing concrete commercialised through his spinout company, Basilisk. In place of quicklime specks, their mix contains dormant, harmless bacteria (Bacillus species) paired with calcium lactate capsules. When a crack lets water seep in, the bacteria wake up, eat the nutrients, and produce solid limestone that seals the gap. Builders across Europe have already poured Basilisk concrete into underground tunnels, water storage tanks, and waterproof basements.

All of these innovations push back against a construction habit built around short-term finance. We tend to design modern infrastructure around financial timelines, building bridges and seawalls to outlast a thirty-to-fifty-year mortgage or government bond and leaving the cost of demolition and rebuilding to the next generation. That cycle comes with a heavy environmental price. Making Portland cement accounts for roughly 7% to 8% of global carbon dioxide emissions, driven both by the fossil fuels burned to heat kilns to 1,450°C and the chemical breakdown of limestone itself during heating (Andrew, 2019). Roman quicklime was fired at much lower temperatures, around 900°C, and the finished concrete slowly absorbed carbon dioxide from the air and sea as it cured.

Looking back at Roman engineering gives us a practical way to clean up modern construction. By pairing ancient self-healing chemistry with modern non-rusting materials, engineers and builders have a real chance to move away from disposable infrastructure and start constructing buildings and harbours meant to endure for centuries.
