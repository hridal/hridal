---
title: "Why Roman Concrete Outlives Modern Infrastructure"
date: "2026-08-23"
category: "other"
summary: "Why concrete the Romans poured into the sea two thousand years ago is still standing, and what modern builders are learning from it."
---

If you walk along almost any modern coastline you will see twentieth-century engineering slowly falling apart, with seawalls crumbling, bridge supports flaking away and concrete piers rusting from the inside out. Most of these structures need major and expensive repairs within fifty to eighty years of being built, which is a surprisingly short life for something made of artificial stone.

Two thousand years ago, Roman engineers poured their own concrete, known as opus caementicium, straight into the Mediterranean Sea to build breakwaters, harbours and vaults that have survived two millennia of crashing waves and seawater while actually growing stronger over time. The gap between that ancient durability and our modern fragility comes down to basic chemistry, kiln temperatures and what our economies choose to reward, since modern concrete is designed for speed and quick returns while Roman concrete was designed to settle into a lasting chemical balance with its surroundings.

Almost everything we build today relies on Ordinary Portland Cement, which was first patented in 1824 by a Leeds bricklayer named Joseph Aspdin and refined into its modern high-temperature form by his son William a couple of decades later. To make it, factories heat limestone and clay in a huge rotating kiln to around 1,450°C, which produces hard lumps called clinker that are then ground into a fine grey powder along with a small amount of gypsum. When this powder is mixed with water it sets quickly into a kind of artificial stone.

On its own, concrete is excellent at handling heavy weights pressing straight down on it, which engineers call compressive strength, although it is very poor at handling stretching or bending forces, which are known as tensile strength. A slab of plain concrete will snap cleanly in half if you try to bend it, so builders stop modern buildings and bridges from failing in this way by embedding a skeleton of steel bars inside the wet concrete.

That pairing of concrete and steel creates a built-in self-destruct mechanism, because fresh Portland cement is naturally very alkaline, with a pH between 12.5 and 13.5, and that alkalinity forms a thin protective film around the steel bars that stops them from rusting. Over a few decades, carbon dioxide from the air slowly seeps into the microscopic pores of the concrete and gradually lowers its pH until that shield is stripped away, and near the coast or on roads treated with winter grit, salt speeds up the damage by attacking the metal directly.

Once the steel starts to rust it expands to between two and six times its original volume, and all that swelling pushes outward with enormous force until the surrounding concrete cracks from the inside. Those cracks then let in even more water and oxygen, which accelerates the rusting further until chunks of concrete eventually break away completely (Broomfield, 2007).

Roman marine concrete avoided this trap because it used an entirely different chemical recipe and contained no metal at all. As the Roman architect Vitruvius recorded in his manual De Architectura around 30 to 15 BCE, Roman builders mixed lime with volcanic ash, specifically a variety called pozzolana from the area around Naples, along with chunks of volcanic rock or broken brick. Instead of setting through a fast reaction with fresh water, Roman concrete cured through a slow chemical reaction between lime, seawater and volcanic glass.

For years scientists assumed that the volcanic ash alone explained why Roman buildings lasted so long, although whenever modern researchers tried to recreate the recipe using standard slaked lime, meaning lime that has been pre-mixed with water into a paste, the concrete never developed the same ability to heal itself.

In 2023, a team led by Admir Masic at MIT uncovered the real mechanism, which they called hot mixing (Seymour et al., 2023). The Romans mixed dry, reactive quicklime directly with the volcanic ash and water, and that combination set off a fierce chemical reaction that generated intense heat and left behind tiny white, chalky specks throughout the concrete called lime clasts. For decades archaeologists had assumed these specks were simply signs of careless mixing, whereas in reality they work like tiny emergency repair kits. Whenever a hairline crack forms, rainwater or seawater seeps in and dissolves the calcium-rich specks, creating a mineral-rich liquid that either hardens into fresh calcium carbonate to plug the gap or reacts with the surrounding volcanic ash to seal the crack shut.

When Roman concrete sits underwater the chemistry becomes even more impressive, as geologist Marie Jackson and her team found when they used high-powered X-rays to inspect core samples drilled from ancient Roman harbours and discovered that seawater actually toughens the concrete as the centuries pass (Jackson et al., 2017).

As seawater slowly dissolves the glassy particles in the volcanic ash, it encourages the growth of rare interlocking crystals, specifically aluminous tobermorite and phillipsite, inside the tiny pores of the concrete. These plate-like crystals weave together like microscopic fibres that stop cracks from spreading and make the concrete much harder to fracture. In modern concrete seawater washes away binding chemicals and corrodes the steel inside, whereas in Roman concrete seawater becomes the very ingredient that keeps the structure curing and healing.

If Roman concrete is so tough and repairs itself so well, it is worth asking why we ever abandoned it, and the answer comes down to construction speed, economics and a serious chemical clash with modern steel.

The first problem is that Roman concrete takes weeks or even months to reach full strength, and its final compressive strength is lower than that of modern structural mixes. Modern construction runs on tight schedules in which contractors want to pour concrete, remove the wooden moulds within days and move straight on to the next floor, which leaves very little room for a material that cures slowly.

The second problem is tensile strength and steel reinforcement, since the Roman mix struggles with stretching and bending forces just as all concrete does. Roman engineers worked around this through geometry, building thick, heavy structures like arches and vaults that kept all the weight pushing straight down, and the unreinforced concrete dome of the Pantheon in Rome still stands after nearly two thousand years because its walls are several metres thick at the base and taper towards the top, where lightweight volcanic pumice keeps the weight down.

Today we want slim skyscrapers and long, sweeping bridges, which require internal reinforcement, and although it might seem possible to simply pour Roman concrete around modern steel bars, the chemistry works against us. The pozzolanic reaction uses up the excess calcium hydroxide that keeps modern concrete highly alkaline, leaving Roman concrete with a much lower pH of around 10 or 11, and at that lower alkalinity standard carbon steel loses its protective film and rusts even faster than usual.

The final problem is simple geography, because good volcanic ash is only found in particular volcanic regions while limestone and clay can be dug up almost anywhere on Earth.

Even so, turning two-thousand-year-old chemistry into something a modern building contractor can actually buy has moved well beyond a laboratory curiosity, and several research teams and start-ups are already doing the groundwork. Following his 2023 study on hot mixing, Admir Masic co-founded a company called DMAT with engineer Paolo Sabatini to bring self-healing Roman concrete back onto commercial building sites. Their aim is to adapt the quicklime-clast recipe so that it sets quickly enough for modern construction schedules while keeping those tiny calcium repair pockets intact, and by adjusting the mix for precast blocks, seawalls and bridge supports they hope to give builders concrete that seals its own hairline cracks before water can do lasting damage.

Other companies are tackling the volcanic ash problem without needing a volcano nearby, since good pozzolana is hard to source globally. Firms such as Urban Mining Industries in the United States have turned to an everyday waste product in crushed bottle glass, which has almost exactly the same glassy silica and alumina make-up as ancient volcanic ash. When it is ground into a fine powder and blended into concrete, it triggers the same slow pozzolanic reaction, soaking up excess calcium hydroxide and sealing off microscopic pores so that seawater struggles to get inside.

Even the best Roman-inspired concrete still needs internal reinforcement to carry heavy loads across long spans, which is where volcanic rock makes a second appearance. Over the past decade, engineers and manufacturers such as ReforceTech in Norway and Technobasalt have been scaling up basalt fibre reinforced polymer rebar, which factories make by melting crushed volcanic basalt at around 1,400°C, drawing the molten stone into continuous hair-thin fibres and binding them together with resin into ribbed bars that look very similar to standard rebar.

Basalt rebar weighs roughly a quarter as much as steel, withstands higher pulling forces before snapping and is completely unaffected by salt and moisture. Because basalt bars never rust they have no need for the highly alkaline environment that Portland cement provides to protect steel, which makes basalt reinforcement a natural partner for lower-pH, Roman-style pozzolanic concrete. Civil engineers in coastal regions, including transport departments in Florida and marine infrastructure researchers at universities such as Queen's University Belfast, have already tested and installed basalt-reinforced concrete in seawalls, bridge decks and coastal piers where salt spray would normally ruin steel bars within a single generation.

Alongside these Roman chemical recipes, researchers are also borrowing ideas from biology to heal cracks. At Delft University of Technology in the Netherlands, microbiologist Henk Jonkers developed a self-healing concrete that is now sold through his spin-out company, Basilisk, and instead of quicklime specks their mix contains dormant, harmless Bacillus bacteria paired with capsules of calcium lactate. When a crack lets water seep in, the bacteria wake up, feed on the nutrients and produce solid limestone that seals the gap, and builders across Europe have already used Basilisk concrete in underground tunnels, water storage tanks and waterproof basements.

All of these innovations push back against a construction habit shaped by short-term finance, in which we tend to design modern infrastructure around financial timelines, building bridges and seawalls to outlast a thirty to fifty-year mortgage or government bond while leaving the cost of demolition and rebuilding to the next generation. That cycle carries a heavy environmental price, since making Portland cement accounts for roughly 7% to 8% of global carbon dioxide emissions, driven both by the fossil fuels burned to heat kilns to 1,450°C and by the chemical breakdown of limestone itself during heating (Andrew, 2019). Roman quicklime, by comparison, was fired at much lower temperatures of around 900°C, and the finished concrete slowly absorbed carbon dioxide from the air and sea as it cured.

Looking back at Roman engineering gives us a practical way to clean up modern construction, and by pairing ancient self-healing chemistry with modern materials that never rust, engineers and builders have a real chance to move away from disposable infrastructure and start building structures and harbours that are meant to last for centuries.
