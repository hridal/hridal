I needed to pick an energy supplier last week and assumed it would take about ten minutes, since all I expected to do was open a comparison site, sort the deals by price and switch to the cheapest one.

Instead I spent three evenings reading Ofgem cost breakdowns and wholesale market guides, because the more I looked the stranger the whole system seemed. Every supplier delivers exactly the same gas and electricity through exactly the same pipes and cables, there is a government price cap, and switching takes a few days without anyone digging up your street or cutting off your power, yet two identical flats on the same landing can end up hundreds of pounds apart depending on which tariff they happen to be on.

That gap matters even more at the moment, because forecasters expect Ofgem's January 2027 price cap to rise by around 26%, pushing a typical annual dual-fuel bill from £1,723 to roughly £2,166 after gas prices climbed in the autumn. Once I started looking into why bills vary so much for what is essentially an identical product, the whole retail energy system began to make a lot more sense.

## 1. What an energy supplier actually does

Your supplier does not run a dedicated cable to your flat or generate the power that boils your kettle, because Britain's energy system is made up of three separate layers. Generators such as offshore wind farms, nuclear reactors, gas power stations, solar arrays and North Sea terminals produce the energy, while regional network monopolies own and maintain the high-voltage pylons, local substations and underground gas pipes that carry it.

Retail suppliers sit on top of that grid as commercial middlemen who forecast how much energy their customers will use, buy it in advance on the wholesale market, manage meters, cover unpaid debt across their customer base and send out the bills. Even tariffs advertised as 100% renewable work in this way, since every home on the local grid receives exactly the same physical mix of power and a green tariff simply means your supplier has matched your annual usage behind the scenes with renewable certificates or contracts with generators.

Once you strip away the branding, what you are really comparing is a pricing structure, a billing and customer support team, and access to smart tariffs if you happen to have the right meter.

## 2. Where your money goes, and why gas still sets electricity prices

Ofgem's breakdown of the £1,723 price cap for October to December 2026 shows what actually sits inside a typical dual-fuel bill. Around 47% goes on wholesale energy, which covers buying the gas and electricity itself along with capacity payments that keep backup power stations on standby for cold, windless evenings. Roughly 24%, or about £417 a year, goes on network costs for maintaining and upgrading transmission lines and local distribution networks, while around 17% covers the supplier's operating, metering and debt costs, including call centres, billing software, smart meter installations and the bad debt that builds up from accounts in arrears. A further 6% or so funds policy and social schemes such as the Warm Home Discount and older renewable subsidies, and Ofgem builds a narrow profit allowance of around 2.7% into the default cap, which works out at about £46 a year on a typical bill. VAT has been temporarily cut to 0% on domestic electricity for the winter of 2026 to 2027, while it remains at 5% on gas.

The strangest part of that breakdown is the way electricity is priced within the wholesale share. Britain now generates a large proportion of its power from renewables, with wind reaching a record 42% of the mix in March 2026 and renewables overall accounting for roughly 45% of generation, and yet UK electricity prices remain among the highest in Europe and rise every time international gas prices climb.

This happens because the wholesale electricity market uses marginal pricing, in which power stations bid into the market in order of how much they cost to run. Wind and solar cost almost nothing to operate once they are built, so they are used first, followed by nuclear, while gas-fired plants have to burn fuel and therefore sit near the top of the queue. The last power station needed to meet national demand in any given half hour sets the wholesale price paid to every generator running at that moment, and because Britain still relies on gas plants to balance the grid most of the time, the cost of burning gas sets the price of electricity even on windy days when turbines are supplying most of the power. Government Contracts for Difference are slowly separating clean generators from gas prices, and wholesale prices fall sharply whenever gas drops below 20% of the mix, although analysts still expect gas to set the UK clearing price around 15% of the time even on a grid that is 95% clean.

## 3. How the price cap works, and what it leaves out

News coverage of the price cap often makes it sound as though Ofgem sets a single national price for energy, whereas in practice it only regulates one particular corner of the market.

Introduced in January 2019 and updated every three months, the cap limits the daily standing charge and the price per unit on default tariffs, which are the standard variable deals you end up on if you never choose a tariff or when a fixed contract ends and rolls over. It places no ceiling on your total bill, which still rises and falls with how much energy you use, and it leaves fixed tariffs completely unregulated, since fixed deals are priced from forward wholesale curves and can therefore sit well below or well above the cap depending on where traders think gas and power prices will go over the coming year.

Even within the cap, comparison sites tend to flatten an important detail, because every tariff is made up of a daily standing charge that you pay simply for being connected to the grid, averaging around 54.8p a day for electricity and 29.7p a day for gas in late 2026, and a unit rate for every kilowatt-hour you use, which is around 26.3p for electricity and 8.0p for gas. Comparison tables usually default to Ofgem's typical household figure of 2,500 kWh of electricity and 9,500 kWh of gas a year, so in a small, well-insulated flat that uses half that amount the standing charge makes up a large share of the annual bill, while in a large, draughty house the unit rate dominates. This means two neighbours looking at the same comparison site can easily end up with completely different cheapest suppliers.

## 4. Why a market selling identical goods runs on customer habits

Around 280,000 to 310,000 households switch electricity supplier in a busy month, while millions of others stay on default tariffs for three years or more, partly because switching feels like admin, partly because people worry about overlapping bills or fixing just before prices fall, and partly because the price cap keeps standard rates close enough to the rest of the market that there is little sense of urgency to move.

Suppliers price around that behaviour, and because Ofgem tightly caps their margins on default tariffs, they save their lowest fixed rates for active switchers in order to win new customers. During the calmer stretches of 2026 the cheapest fixed deals sat roughly £150 to £165 below the default cap, so although staying on a standard variable tariff is far safer than it was before 2019, customers who never switch still end up funding the better deals offered to people who shop around.

## 5. From dozens of challengers back to six big firms

Comparison sites make the retail energy market look crowded, although in reality it has consolidated heavily, with Ofgem counting 22 active domestic suppliers at the start of 2026 and only 17 by the middle of the year, while six companies, namely Octopus Energy, British Gas, E.ON Next, OVO, EDF and ScottishPower, hold roughly 92% of all household accounts.

In the late 2010s dozens of small challengers entered the market with cheap tariffs funded by buying power short-term on the spot market instead of hedging months in advance, and when wholesale gas prices surged in 2021 and 2022 around thirty of those firms collapsed and Ofgem transferred their customers to larger suppliers.

The biggest shift since then has been the rise of Octopus Energy, which was founded in 2016 and overtook the older incumbents to become Britain's largest household supplier, reaching nearly 26% of the market in both electricity and gas by the second half of 2026. Its growth showed that a software-focused newcomer could take a significant share from established utilities, while also leaving a single company with enormous influence over standard pricing and service expectations across the country.

## 6. How smart meters turned electricity into a timing game

Standard fixed tariffs mostly compete within narrow margins, whereas smart time-of-use tariffs price electricity according to when you actually use it.

With a smart meter, suppliers can charge rates linked to what power costs on the grid in every thirty-minute period, so agile tariffs track wholesale prices in real time and occasionally fall below zero during windy nights when supply outstrips demand, while other smart tariffs offer steep overnight discounts aimed at people who charge an electric car or run a home battery.

These tariffs pass wholesale price swings straight onto your daily routine, which means shifting car charging or washing machine cycles to 2am can cut bills noticeably, while households that do most of their cooking and heating between 4pm and 7pm, when gas plants ramp up for the evening peak, often pay more on a time-of-use tariff than they would on a simple flat rate.

## 7. Who the market actually works for

Britain's retail energy market treats different payment methods very differently, and households that pay by Direct Debit, which covers 73% of electricity accounts, have a working smart meter and review their tariff once or twice a year are the ones who benefit from real competition, since fixed deals beat the cap when wholesale markets settle and smart tariffs reward flexible usage.

Customers who pay quarterly when they receive a bill, who make up 16% of electricity accounts, or who use a prepayment meter, who make up 11%, have far fewer options. Ofgem permanently scrapped the old prepayment surcharge in April 2024 so that default prepayment rates now match Direct Debit default rates, removing a long-standing penalty on standard tariffs, although prepayment households remain largely shut out of the cheapest fixed deals and half-hourly smart tariffs. The system therefore protects passive and lower-income households from the worst overcharging while saving its lowest prices for customers who have a smart meter and the flexibility to switch.

## How I'd pick a tariff now

I would start by finding my actual annual usage in kWh and entering my real electricity and gas consumption instead of relying on the comparison site's estimate based on the number of bedrooms, since that is the only way to see whether a lower standing charge or a lower unit rate would save more for my home.

I would then compare fixed deals against where the cap is heading, because ahead of a winter rise like the one expected in January 2027, locking in a fix at or below the current cap protects you from the increase, provided the exit fees are low enough to let you leave if wholesale prices fall in the spring.

After that I would match the tariff to my daily routine, since anyone with an electric car, a home battery or flexible working hours should run the numbers on a time-of-use tariff, whereas a household where everyone gets home at six o'clock and switches everything on at once will usually be better off on a plain flat rate.

Finally I would check Citizens Advice's service rankings before signing up, because every company delivers identical gas and electricity, so accurate billing and how quickly someone answers the phone when a Direct Debit goes wrong are the main differences you will actually notice once you have switched.

Most people spend about ten minutes on a comparison site because energy feels too dull to look into properly, and the entire pricing structure is built around that assumption.
