I needed to pick an energy supplier last week, and I assumed it would take ten minutes: open a comparison site, sort by price and switch.

Instead, I spent three evenings reading Ofgem cost breakdowns and wholesale market guides. But every supplier delivers the exact same gas and electricity through the exact same pipes and cables. There is a government price cap, and switching takes a few days without anyone digging up your street or cutting off your power. Yet two identical flats on the same landing can end up hundreds of pounds apart depending on which tariff they land on.

That gap matters more right now because forecasters expect Ofgem's January 2027 price cap to jump by around 26%, pushing a typical annual dual-fuel bill from £1,723 to roughly £2,166 after autumn gas prices rose. Once I started looking into why bills vary so much for an identical product, the whole retail energy system started making a lot more sense.

## 1. What an energy supplier actually does
Your supplier does not run a dedicated cable to your flat or generate the power boiling your kettle. Britain's energy system has three layers. Generators (offshore wind farms, nuclear reactors, gas power stations, solar arrays and North Sea terminals) produce the energy. Regional network monopolies own and maintain the high-voltage pylons, local substations, and underground gas pipes.

Retail suppliers sit on top of that grid as commercial middlemen. They forecast how much energy their customers will use, buy it ahead of time on the wholesale market, manage meters, cover unpaid debt across their customer base, and send out bills. Even "100% renewable" tariffs work this way. Every home on the local grid receives the exact same physical mix of power; green tariffs simply mean your supplier has matched your annual consumption behind the scenes with renewable certificates or generator contracts.

Once you strip away the branding, you are comparing a pricing structure, a billing and support team, and access to smart tariffs if you have the right meter.

## 2. Where your money goes (and why gas still sets electricity prices)
Ofgem's breakdown of the £1,723 price cap for October to December 2026 shows what actually sits inside a typical dual-fuel bill:
- **Wholesale energy (around 47%):** Buying the gas and electricity itself, plus capacity payments to keep backup power plants on standby for cold, windless evenings.
- **Network costs (around 24%):** Roughly £417 a year goes toward maintaining and upgrading transmission lines and local distribution networks.
- **Supplier operating, metering, and debt costs (around 17%):** Call centres, billing software, smart meter installations, and bad debt built up by accounts in arrears.
- **Policy and social schemes (around 6%):** Funding the Warm Home Discount and older renewable subsidies.
- **Supplier profit margin (around 2.7%):** Ofgem builds a narrow earnings allowance into the default cap, equal to about £46 a year on a typical bill.
- **VAT:** Temporarily cut to 0% on domestic electricity for winter 2026–27, while staying at 5% on gas.

The oddest part of that breakdown is how electricity is priced inside the wholesale slice. Britain generates a large share of its power from renewables (wind hit a record 42% of the mix in March 2026, and renewables overall account for roughly 45% of generation), yet UK electricity prices remain among the highest in Europe, and whenever international gas prices climb, UK electricity bills follow.

That happens because the wholesale electricity market uses marginal pricing. Power stations bid into the market in order of running cost. Wind and solar cost almost nothing to operate once built, so they clear first, followed by nuclear. Gas-fired plants have to burn fuel, which puts them near the top of the stack. The last power station needed to meet national demand in any given half hour sets the wholesale price paid to every generator running at that moment. Because Britain still relies on gas plants to balance the grid most of the time, the cost of burning gas sets the electricity price even on windy days when turbines supply most of the power. Government Contracts for Difference are slowly separating clean generators from gas prices, and wholesale prices drop sharply whenever gas falls below 20% of the mix, though analysts still expect gas to set the UK clearing price roughly 15% of the time even in a 95% clean grid.

## 3. How the price cap works (and what it leaves out)
News coverage of the price cap often makes it sound as if Ofgem sets a single national price for energy. It only regulates a specific corner of the market.

Introduced in January 2019 and updated every three months, the cap limits the daily standing charge and the per-unit rate on default tariffs: the standard variable deals you sit on if you never choose a tariff, or when a fixed contract ends and rolls over. It puts no ceiling on your total bill, which still rises and falls with how much energy you use, and it leaves fixed tariffs completely unregulated. Fixed deals are priced off forward wholesale curves, meaning a fix can sit well below the cap or well above it depending on where energy traders think wholesale gas and power will trade over the coming year.

Even within the cap, comparison sites flatten an important detail. Every tariff has two parts: a daily standing charge paid just for staying connected to the grid (averaging around 54.8p a day for electricity and 29.7p a day for gas in late 2026) and a unit rate per kilowatt-hour used (around 26.3p for electricity and 8.0p for gas). Comparison tables default to Ofgem's "typical household" benchmark of 2,500 kWh of electricity and 9,500 kWh of gas a year. In a small, well-insulated flat that uses half that amount, the standing charge makes up a huge share of the annual bill. In a large, draughty house, the unit rate dominates. Two neighbours looking at the same comparison site can end up with completely different cheapest suppliers.

## 4. Why a market selling identical goods runs on customer habits
Around 280,000 to 310,000 households switch electricity supplier in a busy month, yet millions stay on default tariffs for three years or more. Switching sounds like admin, people worry about overlapping bills or picking a fix right before prices drop, and the price cap keeps standard rates close enough to the wider market that there is little panic to move.

Suppliers price around that behaviour. Because margins on default tariffs are tightly capped by Ofgem, companies reserve their lowest fixed rates for active switchers to win new accounts. During calmer stretches of 2026, the cheapest fixed deals sat roughly £150 to £165 below the default cap. Staying on a standard variable tariff is far safer than it was before 2019, but customers who never switch still end up funding the sharper deals offered to people who shop around.

## 5. From dozens of challengers back to six big firms
Comparison sites make retail energy look crowded, yet the market has consolidated heavily. Ofgem counted 22 active domestic suppliers at the start of 2026, down to 17 by mid-year, with six companies (Octopus Energy, British Gas, E.ON Next, OVO, EDF and ScottishPower) holding roughly 92% of all household accounts.

In the late 2010s, dozens of small challengers entered the market with cheap tariffs funded by buying power short-term on the spot market instead of hedging months ahead. When wholesale gas prices surged in 2021 and 2022, around thirty of those firms collapsed and Ofgem transferred their customers to larger suppliers.

Since then, the biggest shift has been Octopus Energy. Founded in 2016, it overtook the older incumbents to become Britain's largest household supplier, reaching nearly 26% market share in both electricity and gas by the second half of 2026. Its growth showed that a software-focused entrant could take significant share from legacy utilities, while also leaving one company with huge influence over standard pricing and service expectations across the country.

## 6. Smart meters turned electricity into a timing game
Standard fixed tariffs mostly compete within narrow margins. Smart time-of-use tariffs price electricity around when you use it.

With a smart meter, suppliers can charge rates tied to what power costs on the grid every thirty minutes. Agile tariffs track wholesale prices in real time and occasionally drop below zero during windy overnight hours when supply outstrips demand. Other smart tariffs offer steep overnight discounts aimed at drivers charging an EV or households running a home battery.

These tariffs pass wholesale price swings straight onto your daily routine. Shifting car charging or washing machine cycles to 2am cuts bills noticeably. Households that do most of their cooking and heating between 4pm and 7pm, when gas plants ramp up for the evening peak, often pay more on a time-of-use tariff than they would on a simple flat rate.

## 7. Who the market actually works for
Britain's retail energy market treats different payment methods very differently.

Households that pay by Direct Debit (73% of electricity accounts), have a working smart meter, and review their tariff once or twice a year get real competition. Fixed deals beat the cap when wholesale markets settle, and smart tariffs reward flexible usage.

Customers who pay quarterly on receipt of a bill (16% of electricity accounts) or use a prepayment meter (11%) have far fewer options. Ofgem permanently scrapped the old prepayment surcharge in April 2024 so that default prepayment rates match Direct Debit default rates, removing a long-standing penalty on standard tariffs. Even so, prepayment households remain largely locked out of the cheapest fixed deals and half-hourly smart tariffs. The system shields passive and lower-income households from extreme overcharging while saving its lowest prices for customers with a smart meter and the flexibility to switch.

## How I'd pick a tariff now
**Pull your actual annual kWh usage first.** Skip the comparison site's bedroom estimate and enter your real electricity and gas consumption so you can see whether a lower standing charge or a lower unit rate saves more for your home.

**Compare fixed deals against where the cap is heading.** Ahead of a winter rise like January 2027, locking in a fix near or below the current cap shields you from the jump, provided the exit fees are low enough that you can leave if wholesale prices fall in spring.

**Match the tariff to your daily routine.** If you have an EV, a home battery, or flexible hours, run the numbers on a time-of-use tariff. If everyone in your flat gets home at 6pm and turns everything on at once, a plain flat rate usually wins.

**Check Citizens Advice's service rankings before signing.** Since every company delivers identical gas and electricity, accurate billing and how fast someone picks up the phone when a direct debit goes wrong are the main differences you will notice once you sign up.

Most people spend ten minutes on a comparison site because energy feels too boring to investigate, and the entire pricing structure is built around that assumption.
