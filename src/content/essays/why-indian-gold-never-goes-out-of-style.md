---
title: "Why Indian gold never goes out of style"
date: "2026-06-13"
category: "other"
summary: "My grandma's melted-down bangles, a bit of chemistry, and why gold has never really been just a trend."
---

My favourite pieces of jewellery aren't new. A few came straight from my grandma, and some are her old gold, melted down and reshaped into something I'd actually wear now. The metal in a bracelet I put on most mornings has probably been a ring, a necklace and something else before that, worn by women in my family I never met. The design changed every time but the gold stayed the same.

That's the thing I keep coming back to. Fashion is built on things going out of date, such as skinny jeans having a good run, and now they're a way of guessing someone's age. Colours come and go by the season, yet gold has been worn in Indian families for generations, and nobody has ever looked at a gold bangle and thought it was a bit 2015. So why does it never go out of style?

Part of the answer is chemistry, which is the one part I'm meant to be qualified to talk about. Gold is about as unreactive as a metal gets. It doesn't rust, it doesn't tarnish, and it doesn't react with the air or with water, so a piece of gold looks much the same after a hundred years as it did on the first day. Silver goes black and copper goes green, but gold just sits there being gold.

That unreactiveness matters more than it sounds. In a lot of Indian families, girls are only ever given real gold, often from when they're babies: tiny gold studs when their ears are pierced, thin bangles, a chain for a first birthday. Fake jewellery just isn't part of growing up. The result is that plenty of us have spent our whole lives wearing nothing but gold, and then find out the first time we try a cheap pair of earrings that our skin reacts to them. What people call being allergic to fake gold is usually a reaction to the nickel and other base metals underneath the plating. Gold itself is so inert that it hardly ever causes a reaction. So for a lot of Indian girls, real gold isn't just the nicer option. It's the only thing we can comfortably wear.

It's also very soft and very malleable. You can beat it into sheets thinner than paper or draw it into fine wire, which is why Indian goldsmiths could do such detailed work with it. And because it melts at a manageable temperature and doesn't lose anything when it does, you can melt an old piece down and make something completely new without wasting any of it. That's exactly what happened to my grandma's jewellery. The material allows a kind of recycling that very few precious things do. A painting can't be melted into a newer painting.

You can even eat it. The thin shimmering layer on top of some Indian sweets, varq, is silver or gold beaten so fine it's almost see-through. Very few metals would let you do that.

Indian gold also looks different. Most Indian jewellery is 22 carat, which is around 92% gold, while in the UK 9, 14 or 18 carat is far more common. More gold means a deeper, warmer yellow and a softer metal, which is why Indian gold stands out next to Western jewellery, and why every aunty I know can tell real from fake at a glance. Since 2021, gold jewellery sold in India has had to carry a BIS hallmark that shows its purity, which is a very modern answer to a very old worry about whether gold is what it claims to be.

So gold can't wear out and it can always be remade. That already makes it very hard for it to go out of style, because any piece that looks dated can just become a different piece.

Chemistry explains why gold lasts. It doesn't explain why people want it in the first place, and for that you have to look at what it has meant, particularly to women.

In Hindu tradition there's an idea called stridhan, which roughly means a woman's own wealth. The jewellery a bride receives at her wedding is hers, not her husband's family's. For a long time, and in a lot of households, gold was one of the only things a woman truly owned. It couldn't be signed over by someone else, and it was on her body, not in an account in someone else's name.

That makes gold jewellery something more practical than decoration. It's portable savings. If something went wrong, whether a bad harvest, a death in the family or a marriage falling apart, gold could be sold or pawned quickly, almost anywhere, with no bank and no permission needed. My grandma's generation didn't wear gold just because it was pretty. In a way, it was their insurance policy.

Then there's ritual. Gold is bought for weddings, for births and on days that are meant to be auspicious, like Akshaya Tritiya and Dhanteras. When something is tied to the most important moments in people's lives, it stops being a trend. Nobody decides a wedding tradition is out of fashion.

I don't want to make this sound simpler than it is. The same tradition that gave women something of their own also became tangled up with dowry, and with pressure on families to provide a certain amount of gold before a daughter could marry. Gold has been a way for women to hold wealth, but it has also been a way of measuring them. Both things are true, and I think the honest position is that gold gave women some independence inside a system that didn't give them much.

I think gold has never gone out of style because it's never really been just a style. Most fashion is only about how something looks, so when the look changes, it's finished. Gold sits on three things at once: a material that doesn't decay, money you can wear, and meaning that gets passed down. Any one of those would help it last. Having all three is why it seems timeless.

The melting down is the part I find most interesting. People sometimes see it as losing something, as if the old design was the precious part. But I think it proves the opposite. The shape was always the temporary part. What gets handed down is the gold itself, and each generation gets to decide what it should look like. My grandma's bangles became my rings, and one day my rings might become something I can't picture yet.

It also means the gold is much older than any design it's ever been. Because gold doesn't corrode and almost always gets melted down and reused rather than thrown away, nearly all the gold that has ever been mined still exists somewhere, as jewellery, coins or bars. Some of the gold in my grandma's bangles could, in theory, be hundreds of years old. And it goes back much further than that. Most of the gold on Earth is thought to have been made when neutron stars collided, billions of years before the Earth even formed. Something that has lasted that long was never going to be bothered by a change in fashion.

It's part of why I got interested in jewellery that does more than look nice, which is where Jewellness came from. But that's a different essay for another day.
