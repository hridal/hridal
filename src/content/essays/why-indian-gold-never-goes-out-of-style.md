---
title: "Why Indian gold never goes out of style"
date: "2026-06-13"
category: "other"
summary: "My grandma's melted-down bangles, a bit of chemistry, and why gold has lasted through every trend."
---

My favourite pieces of jewellery are far from new, since a few of them came straight from my grandma and others are her old gold, melted down and reshaped into something I would actually wear today. The metal in a bracelet I put on most mornings has probably been a ring, a necklace and something else before that, worn by women in my family whom I never had the chance to meet. Each time it was remade the design changed completely, while the gold itself stayed exactly the same.

That is the thought I keep coming back to, because fashion depends on things going out of date, in the way that skinny jeans had a long run and have now become a fairly reliable way of guessing someone's age. Colours and cuts come and go with every season, yet gold has been worn in Indian families for generations, and I have never heard anyone look at a gold bangle and call it dated. I wanted to understand why gold seems to sit outside that cycle altogether.

Part of the answer lies in chemistry, which happens to be the one part of this I am meant to be qualified to talk about. Gold is about as unreactive as a metal can be, so it does not rust, tarnish or react with air and water, which means a piece of gold looks much the same after a hundred years as it did on the day it was made. Silver slowly turns black and copper gradually turns green, whereas gold simply carries on looking like gold.

That lack of reactivity matters more than it might first seem, because in a lot of Indian families girls are only ever given real gold, often from when they are babies, starting with tiny studs when their ears are pierced, thin bangles and perhaps a chain for a first birthday. Fake jewellery rarely features in that kind of childhood, so many of us grow up wearing nothing except gold and only discover the first time we try a cheap pair of earrings that our skin reacts to them. What people describe as being allergic to fake gold is usually a reaction to the nickel and other base metals hidden underneath the plating, since gold itself is so inert that it almost never causes any irritation. For a lot of Indian girls, then, real gold ends up being the only jewellery they can wear comfortably, as well as the nicest.

Gold is also remarkably soft and malleable, so it can be beaten into sheets thinner than paper or drawn out into fine wire, which is why Indian goldsmiths have always been able to produce such detailed work with it. Because it melts at a manageable temperature and loses nothing in the process, an old piece can be melted down and made into something completely new without any of the metal going to waste, which is exactly what happened to my grandma's jewellery. Very few precious things allow that kind of recycling, in the same way that nobody could melt down an old painting and turn it into a newer one.

It can even be eaten, as the thin shimmering layer on top of some Indian sweets, known as varq, is silver or gold beaten so finely that it is almost see-through, which is something very few metals would allow.

Indian gold also looks noticeably different, because most Indian jewellery is 22 carat, or around 92% gold, while 9, 14 or 18 carat is far more common in the UK. The higher gold content gives a deeper, warmer yellow and a softer metal, which is why Indian gold stands out so clearly next to Western jewellery and why every aunty I know can tell real gold from fake at a glance. Since 2021, gold jewellery sold in India has had to carry a BIS hallmark showing its purity, which offers a very modern answer to the very old worry about whether gold is really what it claims to be.

Gold therefore cannot wear out and can always be remade, which already makes it very difficult for it to fall out of fashion, since any piece that starts to look dated can simply be turned into a different one.

Chemistry explains why gold lasts so well, although it says much less about why people want it in the first place, and for that I think you have to look at what gold has meant over time, particularly to women.

In Hindu tradition there is an idea called stridhan, which roughly translates as a woman's own wealth, and under it the jewellery a bride receives at her wedding belongs to her rather than to her husband's family. For a long time and in a great many households, gold was one of the only things a woman truly owned, since nobody else could sign it away and she carried it on her own body instead of keeping it in an account held in someone else's name.

That makes gold jewellery far more practical than decoration alone, because it works as a form of portable savings that could be sold or pawned quickly, almost anywhere and without needing a bank or anyone's permission, whenever something went wrong, whether that was a bad harvest, a death in the family or a marriage falling apart. My grandma's generation wore gold partly because it was beautiful and partly because, in a very real sense, it served as their insurance policy.

Ritual matters as well, since gold is bought for weddings, for births and on days that are considered auspicious, such as Akshaya Tritiya and Dhanteras. When something becomes tied to the most important moments in people's lives it stops behaving like a trend, in the same way that nobody would ever decide that a wedding tradition had gone out of fashion.

I would rather not make this sound simpler than it really is, because the same tradition that gave women something of their own also became tangled up with dowry and with pressure on families to provide a certain amount of gold before a daughter could marry. Gold has been a way for women to hold wealth while also being used as a way of measuring them, and I think the most honest position is that it gave women some independence within a system that otherwise offered them very little.

In the end I think gold has never gone out of style because it has never really depended on style in the first place, whereas most fashion rests entirely on how something looks and fades as soon as that look changes. Gold rests on three things at once, namely a material that does not decay, money that can be worn and meaning that gets passed down through families, and while any one of those would help it last, the combination of all three is what makes it seem genuinely timeless.

The melting down is the part I find most interesting, because people sometimes treat it as a loss, as though the old design were the precious part, whereas I think it shows the opposite. The shape was always the temporary part, and what really gets handed down is the gold itself, with each generation deciding for itself what that gold should look like. My grandma's bangles became my rings, and one day my rings may well become something I cannot yet picture.

This also means the gold is far older than any design it has taken, because gold does not corrode and is almost always melted down and reused instead of being thrown away, so nearly all the gold that has ever been mined still exists somewhere as jewellery, coins or bars. Some of the gold in my grandma's bangles could, in theory, be hundreds of years old, and its story goes back much further still, since most of the gold on Earth is thought to have formed when neutron stars collided, billions of years before the Earth itself existed. Something that has lasted for that long was never likely to be troubled by a change in fashion.

All of this is part of why I became interested in jewellery that does more than simply look nice, which is where Jewellness eventually came from, although that is a different essay for another day.
